01

Castle health, both builds

Two charts rather than one with two vertical scales: health peaks at 78,000 and incoming damage at 92,090, so a shared axis would flatten one and invent a crossing point that means nothing.

The shape changed, not just the numbers

The old line is a cliff — a repair-funded spike to 78,000 and then 24,045 health gone per second. The current line never gets that high and never falls that fast: its worst moment is 3.6 times shallower (6,750 HP/s), and it grinds down through several similar windows instead of being deleted in one.

Peak incoming damage went the other way — 29,990 to 92,090, 3.1 times worse. An earlier draft of this page read that as the wipes landing, back when the number fell. It rose because the bot now lives 110 seconds longer, and the opponent's economy compounds through every one of them. Surviving longer means meeting a bigger army, so peak DPS is a clock reading, not a scoreboard — the honest measure is the slope of our health, and that is 3.6 times shallower.

03

The economy, both players

Money on hand through the current build's game. The ticks along the bottom mark investments — the sawtooth is the economy laddering up, not spending.

It wins the economic race for 7 rungs and loses it on the eighth

The two ladders are lock-step identical through investment 7 — the same 7 rungs at the same seconds, and we reach the last shared one 4 seconds ahead of them (147s against 151s). The premise of the whole defence-only design — defend cheaply, keep pace, reach Armageddon — is working exactly as intended, right up to the rung that decides the game.

Then they buy investment 8 at 227s, their income triples to $2,500/s, and we never buy it at all. We die 75 seconds later.

The eighth investment costs $40,000. In the 155 seconds we had, we earned $116,400 and spent $84,821 of it on defence — against the $151,559 they spent attacking. Our cash peaked at $27,812: 70% of the price.

Correction. An earlier version of this page called investment 8 "Armageddon, and a guaranteed win". It is not. `GameEngine.Invest` fires Armageddon when the count is already 8 — the ninth purchase — and leaves income alone, so investment 8 is an ordinary economy rung and Armageddon costs $121,221 on top of it. That makes the gap far wider than the paragraph above implies: the bot was not one affordable rung from winning, it was two, and the second is 3x the price of the first.

Our line sits above $5,000 for 52% of the game, up from 16% before the repair fix. That is money not being burned on $8,837 repairs — but the defensive comparison prices a blocker against dying and never against the economy rung it is trying to reach, so the $40,000 it peaked 70% of the way toward stays invisible to it.

Investment 7 is where the game is decided — and it is choosing again

Through investments 3 to 5 the bot is in watch almost the whole time — correctly, nothing is threatening it. At investment 6 it fights its first real engagement and spends most of it holding, which is the branch working as designed: it lets the wave form and answers it.

Investment 7 is then 155 seconds long, half the game, and the whole outcome sits inside it. The previous build spent 68% of that level in critical — not a decision but the absence of one, the survival law holding it permanently inside its own death window so the dollar comparison never ran. It is now 61%, with 5% block, 14% outmatched and 6% wipe — wipes firing 3 times as often as before, at exactly the point where one unit can clear the biggest army it will ever face.

That is the intended machinery finally running under load. It is also still a loss, which is the point worth keeping: the comparison now works and picks the best available option, and the best available option is not good enough.

Across the whole game: 41% idle, 15% holding, 6% spending, 37% critical — so only 6% of the game is spent actually buying anything, and more than a quarter of it is spent past the point where the bot still has a choice.

05

Are we winning the fights?

Castle health cannot answer that — it falls for a while in every game. What answers it is the cumulative value each side has had destroyed. While our line is below theirs we are trading up.

Both sides upgrade Reinforcements at 152s, and that is the whole story

Before it we are comfortably ahead on trades — $3,918 destroyed against $3,168 lost, a ratio of 1.24×. After it we are behind at 0.77×. The economies are lock-step, so both players cross on the same second.

The mechanism is the payload tier, and the stall harness measured it long before this bot existed. reinforcements_2 delivers five tier-5s, which a chump line holds at 2 bodies/second. reinforcements_3 delivers five tier-7s, which needs 30/second. The bot's hard ceiling is 6/second — one purchase per decision. One upgrade moves the incoming wave from three times inside what a blocking defence can hold to five times beyond it, and then repeats every ten seconds forever.

The bot described as a chump-blocker spends 2% of its budget on tier-1 bodies. The survival law that the whole design rests on governs a fraction of what it actually does; the rest goes on high-tier wipers, bought in the same minutes its own reinforcements_3 is delivering the same units free. Those free ones march out and attack, so they are not available to defend — which is the real defect, and it is not one the wiper logic can fix.
